Ancient West African Empires and Natural Resource Discoveries

Ghana Empire • Mali Empire • Songhai Empire

Long before modern borders were drawn, West Africa was home to powerful kingdoms and empires whose influence reached across the Sahara. Their wealth was closely connected to gold, salt and the movement of goods between communities in the south and trading centres in North Africa.

This blog explores the Ghana, Mali and Songhai Empires, the important gold-producing regions linked to their economies, and what historical evidence tells us about their knowledge of natural resources. It also compares these historic resource areas with selected modern gold and oil-producing regions in Africa.

The three great empires

The Ghana Empire (c. 300–1200 CE)

The Ghana Empire was one of the earliest major states in West African history. Located mainly in areas of present-day Mauritania and western Mali, it became an important centre of trans-Saharan commerce. Its capital was Koumbi Saleh, a major settlement associated with the empire.

Gold and salt were especially valuable in this trade. Ghana collected taxes from merchants and caravans moving goods through its territory. Gold came from nearby producing regions, including Bambuk and areas often identified in historical accounts as Wangara. This means the empire’s power depended not only on gold itself, but also on its ability to protect and benefit from trade routes.

The Mali Empire (c. 1235–1600 CE)

The Mali Empire rose after the decline of Ghana and is associated with the leadership of Sundiata Keita, who founded the empire after the Battle of Kirina. Mali later reached great influence under Mansa Musa, whose famous pilgrimage demonstrated the wealth and international connections of his empire.

Gold was central to Mali’s prosperity. The empire benefited from connections to important gold-producing areas, particularly Bure in present-day Guinea, as well as Bambuk and other western regions. Control of trade routes and commercial centres helped rulers collect revenue and strengthen their authority.

The Songhai Empire (c. 1464–1591 CE)

Songhai grew in power after Mali’s influence weakened. Its centre was Gao, and at its height the empire controlled major commercial and learning centres such as Timbuktu and Jenne. Its position along the Niger River and important trade routes helped it connect different parts of West Africa.

Like the earlier empires, Songhai gained wealth from trade. Rulers collected taxes from merchants and caravans, while the empire’s influence over important routes helped goods move between the gold-producing south and markets farther north. Its prosperity therefore depended heavily on trade administration and political control, not simply on owning gold mines.

Major gold-producing regions of West Africa

Several regions contributed to West Africa’s historic gold economy. Bambuk, near the present-day Mali–Senegal border, was one of the oldest known gold-producing areas. Bure, in present-day Guinea, became an important supplier during the Mali Empire. Galam, in Senegal, was another recognised gold region, while the Akan region of present-day Ghana later became one of the most important sources of gold in the area.

These places help explain why gold was so important to the region’s history. However, it is useful to distinguish between a gold-producing region and an empire that controlled the trade passing through it. Political influence over merchants and routes could bring wealth even when mining took place beyond the ruler’s direct control.

Did these empires discover crude oil?

An important question in this topic is whether the Ghana, Mali and Songhai Empires discovered or produced crude oil. The historical evidence presented in this project does not show that any of the three empires explored for or extracted crude oil.

Their economies were centred on resources and activities that were important at the time, including gold, salt, copper, agricultural products and trade. Modern crude-oil exploration depends on geological knowledge and technologies that were not part of these empires’ resource economies. It is therefore more accurate to say that there is no known historical evidence of oil production by these empires, rather than to assume that they had a developed oil industry.

Historic gold regions and modern African resources

The history of these gold fields can be connected to the modern resource map of Africa. Countries such as Ghana, Mali, Guinea and Senegal continue to be associated with gold production. Modern oil-producing areas include Ghana’s Jubilee Field and offshore developments associated with Senegal and Mauritania, as well as the Niger Basin region.

This comparison shows how the natural resources of West Africa remain important, while also reminding us that modern oil exploration belongs to a much later period and uses very different methods and technology.

How gold helped build empires

Gold supported more than the wealth of individual traders. Its movement created opportunities for rulers to collect taxes, support their administrations and strengthen important trading towns. Trade also encouraged contact between communities, linking West Africa with North African markets across the Sahara.

In simple terms, gold helped attract merchants; trade routes brought goods and people together; and rulers who controlled key routes and markets could collect revenue. This relationship between resources, trade and political power is one of the main lessons from the history of these empires.

What does the evidence tell us?

  • Gold was central to the prosperity of the Ghana, Mali and Songhai Empires.
  • Bambuk, Bure, Galam and the Akan region were important gold-producing areas in West African history.
  • The empires gained wealth through trade, taxation and control of important commercial routes.
  • There is no established historical evidence that these empires discovered or produced crude oil.
  • Modern oil discoveries and extraction took place much later, using modern geological methods and technology.
